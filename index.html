Why a Fire Pit

The Difference Between a Patio and a Place People Stay

Most backyards have somewhere to sit. Far fewer have a reason to sit there after eight o'clock. A fire feature is the element that changes that — it gives the seating a focal point to face, it makes the space usable on a cool Southern California evening, and it turns a patio into the part of the house people gravitate to. That is the job modern fire pits and backyard fire pits do, whether the feature ends up freestanding or built into the hardscape.

That is a different job from a pool fire bowl. A fire bowl is architecture: it sits on the pool structure and is admired from across the water. A fire pit or fire table is furniture you gather around — the layout is decided by where the chairs go, not by where the bond beam runs. It is the difference between a feature you look at and one you entertain around.

Get the height right and the seating arranges itself. Get it wrong and you have a beautiful object nobody sits near.

BellaFina Outdoors is more than a place to buy a fire pit. As an outdoor living design center we help homeowners and trade professionals work through height, size, fuel, ignition and finish, so the feature suits the space it is going into — and so it coordinates with the stone, tile, kitchen and shade structures around it.

Gas Fire Pits

Modern Gas Fire Pits for Patios and Backyards

A gas fire pit lights on demand and goes out the same way. There is no wood to store, no embers drifting toward a neighbour's roof and no ash to shovel out the next morning — which is why most Southern California backyards end up on gas. What varies is how the flame is presented: a wide bed of fire glass reads modern and low, a lava rock bed reads warmer and more textured, and a linear trough suits a long seating run better than a round pit does.

Beneath all of them sits the same set of decisions — burner size, fuel type, ignition and enclosure venting — and all four are easier to settle before anything is built.

  • Round, square and linear burner configurations to suit the seating layout
  • Match-lit, push-button spark or fully electronic ignition with remote options
  • Natural gas or propane, with concealed-tank options on freestanding models
  • Fire glass, lava rock and ceramic log media in a range of finishes

Fire Tables

Outdoor Fire Tables: Dining Height, Bar Height, Lounge Height

A fire table is a fire pit built at a working height, and that one change decides how the space gets used. At lounge height it anchors a set of deep-seating chairs. At dining height it becomes the table people eat at, with the flame running down the middle. At bar height it pairs with stools along the edge of a kitchen run or a balcony rail.

Because the top is a usable surface, material matters more here than on a low pit — concrete, GFRC, porcelain and powder-coated aluminium all wear differently under glasses, sun and coastal air. We can walk you through the trade-offs against the setting the table is going into.

  • Lounge, dining and bar heights to match the furniture around them
  • Rectangular, square and round tops, from small balcony sizes upward
  • Concrete, GFRC, porcelain and powder-coated metal finishes
  • Burner covers and lids so the surface stays usable when the flame is off

How to Choose an Outdoor Fire Pit: What to Decide Before You Order

Five decisions shape how a fire feature looks, what it costs and how well it works for hosting. All five are cheaper to settle now than after the patio is poured — and none of them require you to know the jargon. Here is what each one actually means.

1Height & SeatingHow will people sit around it?

Start here. Height decides the furniture, and the furniture decides the size — so this one choice shapes everything after it.

  • Lounge height — roughly coffee-table height, for deep seating and long conversations. This is the classic backyard fire pit.
  • Dining height — a fire table you actually eat around, with the flame running down the middle. Best if the patio has one spot doing double duty.
  • Bar height — pairs with stools along an outdoor kitchen run or a balcony rail. Good where floor space is tight.
2Built-In or FreestandingPart of the patio, or furniture you can move?

Neither is better — they solve different problems and cost very differently.

  • Built-in fire pits can be veneered in the same stone or tile as the hardscape, so the feature reads as part of the architecture. It needs an enclosure, venting and a gas line run to it.
  • Freestanding fire pits and gas fire tables arrive finished, need no construction, and can be moved — or taken with you. Most hide a propane tank in the base.
3Fuel TypeWhat is it going to burn?

This affects burner sizing and the specifications your installer works from, so it is worth settling early.

  • Natural gas — nothing to refill and it never runs out mid-evening. Needs a line run to the location, so it suits a permanent feature you will use often.
  • Propane — goes anywhere a line does not reach, and freestanding models are usually built to conceal the tank.
  • Wood — the crackle and the smell, which some people specifically want. Check your city's rules on open burning and air quality first.
4Burner, Media & IgnitionWhat should the flame look like — and how do you light it?

These three choices are what make one gas fire pit look modern and another look rustic.

  • Burner shape — a wide round bed, a square, or a linear trough down a bench run. Linear suits long seating; round suits a circle of chairs.
  • Fire media — fire glass reads modern and low, lava rock reads warmer and more textured, ceramic logs read traditional.
  • Ignition — match-lit is simplest and cheapest; a push-button spark is the middle ground; electronic ignition with a remote or wall switch is the one you will actually use on a Tuesday. Match it to how often you expect to light it.
5Clearances & PlacementWhere can it safely go?

The one people discover too late. Every product publishes minimum distances, and they should shape the layout from the start.

  • Clearance to combustibles and to seating is published for each product — those figures decide how far back the chairs sit.
  • Enclosures have their own venting requirements, which is why a built-in cannot simply be sealed up.
  • Anything overhead needs extra care — pergolas, shade sails and covered patios all change what is allowed.

Not sure where you land on any of these? That is what the consultation is for — send a photo of the space and we will walk you through the five in plain English. Get a free quote →

Fire Pit Questions

Common Questions About Outdoor Fire Pits & Fire Tables

What's the difference between a fire pit and a fire table?

Height, and how you end up sitting around it. A fire pit sits low, at roughly coffee-table height, so lounge seating gathers around it and people lean in. A fire table is built at dining or bar height, so it doubles as a surface for glasses and plates and works with normal chairs or stools. The burner underneath is often identical — what changes is how the space around it gets used.

How much does a gas fire pit cost?

It depends almost entirely on whether it is freestanding or built in. Freestanding gas fire tables typically land somewhere around $700 to $2,500 depending on size and materials. A custom built-in fire feature is a different kind of project: industry figures commonly put mid-range permanent installations in the $3,000 to $7,500 range once you add the enclosure, veneer, burner and ignition. Gas line work is usually quoted separately, often around $14 to $25 per linear foot, and permits where required commonly run $150 to $500. Those are general market ranges rather than our pricing — tell us which direction you are leaning and we will give you a real figure to plan against.

Do I need a permit for a gas fire pit?

Usually yes for anything permanent, and usually no for anything portable. A freestanding fire table you unbox and connect a tank to generally needs no permit. A built-in fire feature does, and in particular any new natural gas line — the gas piping is normally what triggers it. Requirements vary by city and county across Southern California, so confirm with your local building department before work starts. We supply the manufacturer specifications and rough-in information your contractor needs for the plan set.

How far does a fire pit need to be from the house?

Follow the clearance printed in your product's manual — that figure is what actually governs, and it varies by model. As a rough guide, gas fire pits are listed with far smaller clearances than wood, often a few feet to combustible surfaces, while wood-burning pits are commonly kept ten to twenty-five feet from structures because of embers. Clearance to seating is published too, and it is the number that quietly decides how far back the chairs sit. We check these against your patio dimensions before anything is ordered.

Can I put a fire pit under a covered patio or pergola?

Sometimes for gas, almost never for wood. A wood-burning pit under any roof is a genuine hazard — embers and smoke have nowhere to go. A gas fire pit may be permitted under an open or partially covered structure, but only where the manufacturer lists it and only with the overhead clearance they specify above the flame. Solid roofs, wood-beam pergolas and shade sails all need particular attention, because radiant heat can damage the structure over time even when nothing ignites. Check the product listing and your local code before committing to the location.

How many BTUs do I need for a fire pit or fire table?

Most outdoor fire tables run between roughly 30,000 and 65,000 BTU, and 40,000 to 55,000 is the range that gives a strong, visible flame with comfortable warmth at seating distance. Going higher mainly matters in open, windy or exposed settings. Bear in mind that a bigger burner also empties a propane tank faster, so the highest BTU figure is not automatically the right one.

What size fire table do I need to seat six people?

A rectangular fire table in the 48 to 60 inch range typically seats four to six and suits most standard patios. Proportion matters more than raw dimensions, though: a feature that is too small disappears on a large patio, and one that is too large pushes the seating back against the walls. We size it against your actual patio dimensions and furniture plan rather than a catalogue page.

Natural gas or propane — which is better for an outdoor fire pit?

Natural gas suits a permanent, frequently used feature — nothing to refill and it never runs out mid-evening — but it needs a line run to the location. Propane goes anywhere no line reaches, and most freestanding fire tables are built to conceal a tank in the base. The decision affects burner sizing and the specifications your installer works from, so it is worth settling before the burner is ordered.

How long does a propane tank last in a fire table?

A standard 20 lb tank generally gives somewhere around 8 to 12 hours on high, and 15 to 20 hours turned down. The figure moves with the burner: a high-BTU table empties a tank noticeably faster than a modest one. If you expect to use the feature several evenings a week, that arithmetic is often what pushes people toward running a natural gas line instead.

Can I convert a propane fire pit to natural gas?

Only where the manufacturer offers a conversion kit for that specific model, and it is not a matter of swapping an orifice yourself. The two fuels run at different pressures and need different burner sizing, so an improvised conversion is genuinely unsafe. Tell us which fuel you will be on before you order and we will spec it correctly the first time.

Do gas fire pits actually give off much heat?

Yes, though ambience is the main event. A feature in the region of 50,000 BTU throws noticeable radiant warmth roughly eight to ten feet out, which is enough to make a patio comfortable on a cool Southern California evening. It warms the people around it rather than the whole yard, and wind and how open the space is make a real difference.

Can you roast marshmallows over a gas fire pit?

Check the manufacturer's guidance, because many say no. Drippings from food fall into the burner ports and the fire media, where sugar and fat bake on and eventually cause uneven flame or blockages. Some products are built to tolerate it and some explicitly are not. If s'mores matter to you, say so early and we will point you at features that allow it, or at a wood-burning option.

Can a fire pit be built into an existing patio?

Often, yes. A built-in fire pit needs a stable base, a gas supply routed to it and proper venting in the enclosure. On existing hardscape that usually means trenching for the gas line and building the enclosure on top, which your contractor can confirm on site. Where trenching is not practical, a freestanding gas fire table with a concealed tank gets you a similar result with no excavation.

Are gas fire pits better than wood-burning ones?

For most Southern California backyards gas is the practical choice: it lights instantly, produces no embers or smoke to drift into a neighbour's yard, and leaves no ash to shovel out. Wood delivers the crackle and the smell that some people specifically want. Local rules on open burning and air quality vary by city, so check what applies at your address before settling on wood.

What maintenance does a gas fire pit need?

Light, on the whole. Keep the burner and its ports clear of debris, rinse and re-level the fire media occasionally, and cover the feature when it will sit unused through a wet stretch. Have the ignition and gas connections checked periodically, and follow the manufacturer's guidance for your specific product.

Do you supply fire pits to landscape contractors and designers?

Yes. We work with landscape contractors, hardscape installers and designers throughout Southern California — trade pricing, manufacturer specifications and rough-in information for plan sets, burner and ignition components matched to the enclosure, and deliveries coordinated with your build calendar.

We factor both in when specifying.
